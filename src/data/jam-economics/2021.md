## Q1
meta: MCQ | 1 | A
tags: Macroeconomics | Short-run income determination (IS–LM)
When the expected future marginal product of capital increases, then the IS curve
(A) shifts up and to the right
(B) shifts down and to the left
(C) becomes steeper
(D) becomes flatter
---
**Basics first.** The IS curve plots all (income \(Y\), interest rate \(r\)) pairs where the goods market clears (\(Y = C + I + G\)). Anything that raises spending at every interest rate shifts the whole curve outward (up and to the right). A change in how sensitive spending is to \(r\) changes the slope instead.

**Solution.** Firms invest more when capital is expected to be more productive in future. So investment \(I\) is higher at every \(r\), total demand is higher at every \(r\), and the IS curve shifts up and to the right. The sensitivity of investment to \(r\) has not changed, so the slope is unchanged.

**Answer: (A)**

## Q2
meta: MCQ | 1 | A
tags: Macroeconomics | Unemployment and inflation
An unanticipated inflation would cause
(A) redistribution of wealth from lenders to borrowers
(B) redistribution of wealth from borrowers to lenders
(C) gains for both borrowers and lenders
(D) losses for both borrowers and lenders
---
**Basics first.** Loans are written in money terms at a fixed interest rate. If prices turn out higher than expected, the money that the borrower repays buys less than expected. (The real interest rate = nominal rate − inflation.)

**Solution.** Surprise inflation lowers the real value of what the borrower owes. The borrower repays with "cheaper" money, so the borrower gains and the lender loses. Wealth moves from lenders to borrowers. (Fully anticipated inflation is built into the nominal rate and causes no such transfer.)

**Answer: (A)**

## Q3
meta: MCQ | 1 | C
tags: Statistics | Descriptive statistics
Let $\{x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\}$ be the realization of a randomly drawn sample of size $n$ with sample mean $\bar x$, and $k$ be a real number other than $\bar x$. Let $S_1$ and $S_2$ be the sums of squared deviations defined as $S_1 = \sum (x_i - \bar x)^2$ and $S_2 = \sum (x_i - k)^2$. Then,
(A) $S_1 > S_2$
(B) $S_1 > S_2$ only if $\bar x < k$
(C) $S_1 < S_2$
(D) $S_1 > S_2$ only if $\bar x > k$
---
**Basics first.** The mean is the number that makes the sum of squared deviations as small as possible (the "least squares" property). Algebraic proof: add and subtract \(\bar x\) inside the bracket.

**Solution.**
\[
S_2 = \sum[(x_i - \bar x) + (\bar x - k)]^2 = \sum(x_i - \bar x)^2 + 2(\bar x - k)\sum(x_i - \bar x) + n(\bar x - k)^2.
\]
The middle term is zero because deviations from the mean sum to zero. So \(S_2 = S_1 + n(\bar x - k)^2\). Since \(k \ne \bar x\), the last term is strictly positive, so \(S_2 > S_1\), i.e. \(S_1 < S_2\), whatever the sign of \(\bar x - k\).

**Answer: (C)**

## Q4
meta: MCQ | 1 | B
tags: Microeconomics | Consumer theory
You have a budget of Rs. 4000 and would like to purchase LPG cylinders from a local seller who charges Rs. 50 per cylinder. The seller has a subsidy scheme by which if you return the empty cylinder purchased from him, you will get a refund of Rs. 20 per cylinder. You cannot borrow money from anyone. (A) 131
(B) 132
(C) 133
(D) 134
---

**Solution (the shortcut).** Suppose you end up buying \(n\) cylinders and return \(n - 1\) of them (the last one you keep). Net cash spent \(= 50n - 20(n - 1) = 30n + 20\). Need \(30n + 20 \le 4000\), so \(n \le 132.67\), so \(n = 132\).

Return 5, get Rs 100 (cash 130), buy 2 (cash 30). Return 2, get Rs 40 (cash 70), buy 1 (cash 20). Total \(= 80 + 32 + 12 + 5 + 2 + 1 = 132\). ## Q8

The differential equation $(3x^2y + y^3)\,dx + (x^3 + 3xy^2)\,dy = 0$ is
(A) homogenous and exact
(B) neither separable nor exact
(C) exact and not homogenous
(D) homogenous and not exact
---
**Basics first.** Write the equation as \(M\,dx + N\,dy = 0\).
- **Exact** if \(\partial M/\partial y = \partial N/\partial x\).

## Q11
meta: MCQ | 2 | D
tags: Microeconomics | Imperfect competition
A monopoly canteen serves packed meals to two groups of consumers, group X and group Y. The demand for packed meals for X and Y are given by $Q_X = 200 - 4P$ and $Q_Y = 300 - P$, where $P$ is the uniform price per unit. The unit cost of producing each meal is Rs. 50. The value of $P$ (in Rs.) that maximizes the canteen's profit is
(A) 75
(B) 50
(C) 125
(D) 175
---
**Basics first.** A monopolist maximises profit = (P − unit cost) × total quantity. With a uniform price, the quantity is the sum of both groups' demands, but a group's demand cannot be negative: \(Q_X = 200 - 4P\) hits zero at \(P = 50\), and above that price group X buys nothing.

**Solution.**
- For \(P \ge 50\) only group Y buys: \(Q = 300 - P\). Profit \(= (P - 50)(300 - P)\).
- Maximise: \(d/dP = (300 - P) - (P - 50) = 350 - 2P = 0\), so \(P = 175\). Profit \(= 125 \times 125 = 15{,}625\).
- For \(P < 50\) both groups buy, but the price is below the unit cost of 50, so every meal sold makes a loss. That cannot beat the profit above.

So the best uniform price is 175. (Trap: adding both demands and using \(Q = 500 - 5P\) would give 75, but that wrongly lets group X buy a negative quantity above \(P = 50\).)

**Answer: (D) 175**

(A) 5

(A) $\frac{\sigma_1}{\sigma_1+\sigma_2}\hat\alpha_1 + \frac{\sigma_2}{\sigma_1+\sigma_2}\hat\alpha_2$

## Q21
meta: MCQ | 2 | C
tags: Mathematics | Preliminaries and functions
Let $f, g : \mathbb R \to \mathbb R$ be defined by $f(x) = xe^{-x}$ and $g(x) = x|x|$. Then, on $\mathbb R$,
(A) both $f$ and $g$ are convex
(B) $f$ is convex and $g$ is not convex
(C) $f$ is not quasiconvex and $g$ is quasiconvex
(D) neither $f$ nor $g$ is quasiconvex
---
**Basics first.**
- **Convex function:** \(f''(x) \ge 0\) everywhere (it curves upward).
- **Quasiconvex function:** every lower contour set \(\{x : f(x) \le c\}\) is a convex set (an interval). Any monotonic (always rising or always falling) function of one variable is quasiconvex. A function that rises then falls (a hump) is not quasiconvex.

**Solution.**
- \(f(x) = xe^{-x}\): \(f' = e^{-x}(1 - x)\) and \(f'' = e^{-x}(x - 2)\). \(f'' < 0\) for \(x < 2\), so \(f\) is not convex. \(f\) rises on \((-\infty, 1)\) and falls on \((1, \infty)\): a hump with \(f(x) \to 0\) as \(x \to \infty\). For a small positive \(c\), \(\{f \le c\} = (-\infty, x_1] \cup [x_2, \infty)\), two separate pieces, so \(f\) is not quasiconvex.
- \(g(x) = x|x| = x^2\) for \(x \ge 0\) and \(-x^2\) for \(x < 0\). \(g'(x) = 2|x| \ge 0\), so \(g\) is always increasing, hence quasiconvex. (\(g'' = 2\cdot\text{sign}(x)\) is negative for \(x < 0\), so \(g\) is not convex.)

So \(f\) is not quasiconvex and \(g\) is quasiconvex.

**Answer: (C)**

## Q22

tags: Mathematics | Optimization
Let $(x_1^* = 1, x_2^* = 0, x_3^* = 2)$ be an optimal solution of the linear programming problem: minimize $x_1 + 5x_2 + 2x_3$ subject to $x_1 - x_2 \le 1$, $x_1 + x_2 + x_3 \ge 3$, $x_1, x_2, x_3 \ge 0$. If $(\lambda_1^*, \lambda_2^*)$ is an optimal solution of its dual, then

(B) $2\lambda_1^* = \lambda_2^*$

(D) $\lambda_1^* = \lambda_2^*$

**Basics first.** Every LP has a dual. Each constraint of the primal gets one dual variable \(\lambda\) (a "shadow price"). **Solution.**
- Rewrite the first constraint in "≥" form: \(-x_1 + x_2 \ge -1\). Then the dual is: maximise \(-\lambda_1 + 3\lambda_2\) subject to \(-\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \le 1\) (for \(x_1\)), \(\lambda_1 + \lambda_2 \le 5\) (for \(x_2\)), \(\lambda_2 \le 2\) (for \(x_3\)), \(\lambda_1, \lambda_2 \ge 0\).
