## Q1
meta: MCQ | 1 | A
tags: Statistics | Hypothesis testing
Which one of the following is a non-parametric test?
(A) $\chi^2$-test
(B) $t$-test
(C) $F$-test
(D) $z$-test
---
**Basics first.** A parametric test assumes the data come from a known distribution (usually Normal) and tests a parameter such as the mean or variance. A non-parametric test makes no such assumption about the population's distribution.

**Solution.** The t-test and z-test test means and need a Normal population (or a large sample). The F-test compares two variances and also needs Normality. The χ² goodness-of-fit and independence tests only compare observed counts with expected counts, so they do not assume a distribution.

**Answer: (A)**

## Q2
meta: MCQ | 1 | C
tags: Microeconomics | Consumer theory
Let $x$ and $y$ be two consumption bundles, assumed to be non-negative and perfectly divisible. Further, the assumptions of completeness, transitivity, reflexivity, non-satiation, continuity, and strict convexity are satisfied. Then, which of the following statements is NOT CORRECT?
(A) Either $x \succsim y$, or $y \succsim x$, or both
(B) $y \succ x$ if $y$ contains more of at least one good and no less of any other
(C) $x$ is not indifferent to itself
(D) For $x$ (or $y$), its better set is strictly convex
---
**Basics first.** \(\succsim\) means "at least as good as". The assumptions mean:

- **Strict convexity:** averages are strictly preferred to extremes, so the better set is strictly convex: (D).

**Solution.** (A), (B) and (D) are direct restatements of the assumptions. (C) says \(x\) is not indifferent to itself, which contradicts reflexivity.

**Answer: (C)**

## Q3
meta: MCQ | 1 | A
tags: Microeconomics | Production and cost
Consider a production function of the form $Y = a\log L + (1 - a)\log K$, $a \in (0, 1)$, $a \ne 0.5$, where $Y$ is output, $L$ is labour, and $K$ is capital. Then, the absolute value of elasticity of substitution is
(A) 1
(B) $a$
(C) $(1 - a)$
(D) $\infty$
---
**Basics first.** The elasticity of substitution \(\sigma\) = % change in \((K/L)\) ÷ % change in MRTS. It tells how easily capital can replace labour. Cobb-Douglas gives \(\sigma = 1\), perfect substitutes give \(\infty\), and fixed proportions give 0.

**Solution.** \(MRTS = MP_L / MP_K = (a/L) / ((1-a)/K) = a\cdot K / ((1-a)\cdot L)\). Rearranging, \(K/L = ((1-a)/a) \times MRTS\). Taking logs, \(\ln(K/L) = \text{constant} + 1 \times \ln(MRTS)\), so the slope is 1. (This function is just the log of a Cobb-Douglas function, which is why \(\sigma = 1\).)

**Answer: (A) 1**

## Q11
meta: MCQ | 2 | D
tags: Statistics | Probability theory
Two distinct integers are chosen randomly from 5 consecutive integers. If the random variable $X$ represents the absolute difference between them, then the mean and variance of $X$ are, respectively,
(A) 1 and $\tfrac32$
(B) 2 and 5
(C) 1 and 3
(D) 2 and 1
---
**Basics first.** For a random variable \(X\) taking values \(x\) with probabilities \(p\): Mean \(E(X) = \sum x\cdot p\), and Variance \(= E(X^2) - [E(X)]^2\).

**Solution.** Take the integers 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. The number of ways to pick 2 of them is \(\binom52 = 10\) equally likely pairs. Count the pairs by difference:

| Difference X | Pairs | Count | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | (1,2) (2,3) (3,4) (4,5) | 4 | 4/10 |
| 2 | (1,3) (2,4) (3,5) | 3 | 3/10 |
| 3 | (1,4) (2,5) | 2 | 2/10 |
| 4 | (1,5) | 1 | 1/10 |

**Answer: (D) mean 2, variance 1**

## Q13
meta: MCQ | 2 | B
tags: Mathematics | Optimization
The optimal value of the linear programming problem: Maximise $Z = 2x + 3y$ subject to $5x + 4y \le 20$, $3x + 5y \le 15$, $2x + y \le 4$, $x, y \ge 0$, is
(A) 4
(B) $\tfrac{64}{7}$
(C) 9
(D) $\tfrac{72}{7}$
---
**Basics first.** In a linear programme the best value always occurs at a corner (vertex) of the feasible region. So find the corners and compare \(Z\) at each.

**Solution.**
- Axis intercepts: \(2x + y = 4\) gives \(x \le 2\) when \(y = 0\) (the tightest x-limit, since \(5x \le 20\) gives \(x \le 4\) and \(3x \le 15\) gives \(x \le 5\)). On the y-axis, \(3x + 5y = 15\) gives \(y \le 3\) (tighter than 4 and 5). So corners \((0,0)\), \((2,0)\), \((0,3)\).
- The two constraints \(2x + y = 4\) and \(3x + 5y = 15\) cross. From the first, \(y = 4 - 2x\). Substitute: \(3x + 20 - 10x = 15\), so \(x = 5/7\) and \(y = 18/7\). Check the third constraint: \(5(5/7) + 4(18/7) = 97/7 \approx 13.9 \le 20\), so this point is feasible.
- Evaluate \(Z\): \((0,0)\) gives 0; \((2,0)\) gives 4; \((0,3)\) gives 9; \((5/7, 18/7)\) gives \(10/7 + 54/7 = 64/7 \approx 9.14\).

## Q14
meta: MCQ | 2 | B
tags: Mathematics | Differential & difference equations
The solution of the differential equation $xy\,dx - (x^2 + y^2)\,dy = 0$, $y(0) = 1$ is
(A) $y = e^{x/y}$
(B) $y^2 = e^{x^2/y^2}$
(C) $y^2 = e^{x/y}$
(D) $y = e^{x^2/y^2}$
---
**Basics first.** A homogeneous first-order equation is one where, if you replace \(x\) and \(y\) by \(tx\) and \(ty\), the ratio \(dy/dx\) does not change. The standard trick is to substitute \(x = v\cdot y\) (or \(y = v\cdot x\)), which turns it into a separable equation (variables on separate sides) that you can integrate.

**Solution.** Write \(dx/dy = (x^2 + y^2)/(xy)\). Put \(x = v\cdot y\), so \(dx/dy = v + y\cdot dv/dy\). Then:
\[
v + y\frac{dv}{dy} = \frac{v^2y^2 + y^2}{vy^2} = v + \frac1v
\]
So \(y\cdot dv/dy = 1/v\), i.e. \(v\,dv = dy/y\). Integrate: \(v^2/2 = \ln y + C\). Put back \(v = x/y\): \(x^2/(2y^2) = \ln y + C\).

Use \(y(0) = 1\): \(0 = \ln 1 + C\), so \(C = 0\). Hence \(2\ln y = x^2/y^2\), i.e. \(y^2 = e^{x^2/y^2}\).

**Answer: (B)**

## Q16
meta: MCQ | 2 | A
tags: Microeconomics | Game theory
In a two-player game, player 1 can choose either M or N as strategies. Player 2 can choose either X, Y, or Z as strategies. The payoff matrix (player 1, player 2) is: (M, X) = (3, 1), (M, Y) = (0, 0), (M, Z) = (−1, 2); (N, X) = (0, 0), (N, Y) = (1, 3), (N, Z) = (0.5, 1). Which set of strategy profiles survives iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies?
(A) (N, Y)
(B) (M, X)
(C) (N, Z)
(D) (M, Z)
---
**Basics first.** A strategy is strictly dominated if another strategy gives a strictly higher payoff against every choice of the opponent. A rational player never plays it, so delete it, then repeat on the smaller game.

**Solution.**
- **Player 1** (compare rows): M vs N against X: 3 vs 0; against Y: 0 vs 1; against Z: −1 vs 0.5. Neither row beats the other everywhere, so nothing is deleted yet.
- **Player 2** (compare columns, using player 2's payoffs): X gives (1, 0) against (M, N); Z gives (2, 1). Z beats X in both cases (2 > 1 and 1 > 0), so X is strictly dominated by Z. Delete X.
- Game now has columns Y, Z. **Player 1:** against Y, M gets 0 and N gets 1; against Z, M gets −1 and N gets 0.5. N is better in both, so M is dominated. Delete M.
- Only row N remains. **Player 2** compares Y (payoff 3) with Z (payoff 1) and picks Y, so Z is dominated. Delete Z.

Only (N, Y) survives.

**Answer: (A)**

## Q17
meta: MCQ | 2 | C
tags: Microeconomics | Imperfect competition
For a profit maximising monopolist, the ratio of the profit margin to price (also known as the Lerner Index or the relative mark-up) has a relationship with the price-elasticity of demand at the profit maximising price. Then, which of the following statements is CORRECT?
(A) The larger the elasticity of demand at the profit maximising price, the greater is the relative mark-up
(B) The power to sustain a price higher than the marginal cost depends only on the profit maximising price
(C) At the profit maximising price, given costs are greater than zero, the price elasticity of demand is strictly larger than unity
(D) At the revenue maximising price, the price elasticity of demand is greater than unity
---
**Basics first.** Marginal revenue of a firm facing demand is \(MR = P(1 - 1/|e|)\), where \(|e|\) is the absolute price elasticity. The profit-maximising rule is \(MR = MC\). Rearranging gives the **Lerner Index**:
\[
\frac{P - MC}{P} = \frac{1}{|e|}.
\]

**Solution.**
- **(A) False.** Since the mark-up equals \(1/|e|\), a larger elasticity gives a smaller mark-up (firms with elastic demand have little market power).
- **(B) False.** Market power depends on the elasticity of demand, not just on the price.
- **(C) True.** If \(MC > 0\) then \(MR = MC > 0\), and \(MR = P(1 - 1/|e|) > 0\) requires \(1/|e| < 1\), i.e. \(|e| > 1\). A monopolist with positive costs always prices in the elastic part of demand.
- **(D) False.** Revenue is maximised where \(MR = 0\), i.e. \(1 - 1/|e| = 0\), so \(|e| = 1\) exactly, not greater than 1.

**Answer: (C)**

**Exam takeaway:** Lerner index \(= 1/|e|\); a profit-maximising monopolist with \(MC > 0\) never operates on the inelastic part of demand.

## Q19
meta: MCQ | 2 | D
tags: Microeconomics | Market failure & government policy
Suppose high quality and low quality products are sold at the same price to the buyers. The buyers have less information to determine the quality of the product compared to the sellers at the time of purchase. Which of the following problems arises in this situation?
(A) Moral hazard problem
(B) Market signaling problem
(C) Principal-agent problem
(D) Adverse selection problem
---
**Basics first.** Asymmetric information problems differ by *when* the information gap matters:
- **Adverse selection:** hidden information **before** the transaction (one side knows the quality or type; the other does not).
- **Moral hazard:** hidden **action after** the transaction (e.g. an insured driver drives carelessly).
- **Signalling:** a *solution* to adverse selection, where the informed side takes a costly action (warranty, education) to reveal its type.
- **Principal–agent:** an agent's actions cannot be fully monitored by the principal (a moral-hazard setting).

**Solution.** Here sellers know the quality at the time of purchase and buyers do not, and good and bad products are pooled at one price. This is Akerlof's "market for lemons": good-quality sellers are underpaid and tend to leave, so average quality falls. That is **adverse selection**.

**Answer: (D)**

**Exam takeaway:** Hidden information before the deal → adverse selection; hidden action after the deal → moral hazard.

- **(D, L) = (−c, 0):** Player 1 switching to U gets \(0 > -c\). Not NE.
- **(D, R) = (1−c, 1−c):** Player 1 switching to U gets \(0 < 1-c\); player 2 switching to L gets \(0 < 1-c\) (since \(c < 1\)). **NE.**

There are 2 pure-strategy Nash equilibria. (This is a coordination game: both players prefer to coordinate on D–R, but U–L is also stable.)

**Answer: (B)**

**Exam takeaway:** Always check every cell; coordination games typically have two pure equilibria.
