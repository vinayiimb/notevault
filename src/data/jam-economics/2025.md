## Q1
meta: MCQ | 1 | A
tags: Statistics | Descriptive statistics
For a positively skewed frequency distribution, ______.
(A) Mean > Median > Mode
(B) Mean < Median < Mode
(C) Mode > Mean > Median
(D) Median > Mode > Mean
---
### Explanation from zero
**Skewness** describes the lack of symmetry of a distribution.
- In a **symmetric** distribution, Mean = Median = Mode.
- In a **positively (right) skewed** distribution, a few very large values form a long tail on the right.

The **mean** uses every value, so the few very large values pull it furthest to the right. The **median** is the middle value, so it moves only a little. The **mode** is the peak of the distribution, which stays on the left where most observations are.

So, for positive skewness:
\[
\text{Mean} > \text{Median} > \text{Mode}
\]
(For negative skewness the order reverses: Mean < Median < Mode.)

A useful check is Karl Pearson's empirical relation: \(\text{Mode} \approx 3\,\text{Median} - 2\,\text{Mean}\).

**Correct answer: (A)**

**Remember:** The mean always follows the tail.

## Q4
meta: MCQ | 1 | B
tags: Statistics | Descriptive statistics
Which one of the following is distinct from the others?
(A) Histogram
(B) Pictogram
(C) Ogive
(D) Frequency polygon
---
### Explanation from zero
Statistical data can be shown with **diagrams** or **graphs**.
- **Histogram:** adjacent rectangles whose areas show the frequencies of class intervals. It is a graph of a frequency distribution.
- **Ogive:** the cumulative frequency curve of a frequency distribution.
- **Frequency polygon:** a line joining the mid-points of the tops of the histogram bars. Also a graph of a frequency distribution.
- **Pictogram:** shows data using pictures or symbols (for example, one car symbol = 1,000 cars). It is a **diagram**, not a graph of a frequency distribution.

So the histogram, ogive and frequency polygon belong together, and the pictogram is the odd one out.

**Correct answer: (B)** — Pictogram.

**Remember:** Histogram, frequency polygon and ogive are graphs of frequency distributions; pictograms are pictorial diagrams.

## Q5
meta: MCQ | 1 | A
tags: Microeconomics | Perfect competition & partial equilibrium
Which one of the following is the shut-down condition for a profit maximizing firm in a perfectly competitive market? P- Price, AVC- Average Variable Cost, ATC- Average Total Cost
(A) P < AVC
(B) P > AVC
(C) P < ATC
(D) P > ATC
---
### Explanation from zero
In the short run, a firm has **fixed costs** (rent, machines) that must be paid whether it produces or not, and **variable costs** (labour, raw materials) that it pays only if it produces.

- If the firm **shuts down**, its loss equals its fixed cost.
- If it **produces**, it earns revenue \(P\times Q\) and pays variable cost \(AVC\times Q\) as well as the fixed cost.

Producing is better than shutting down as long as revenue covers variable cost:
\[
P\times Q \ge AVC\times Q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad P \ge AVC.
\]
If \(P < AVC\), every unit produced adds to the loss, so the firm should shut down. The minimum point of AVC is called the **shut-down point**.

Note: \(P < ATC\) means the firm is making a loss, but it should still produce in the short run if \(P \ge AVC\), because it covers part of its fixed cost.

**Correct answer: (A)** — \(P < AVC\).

**Remember:** Short-run shut-down: \(P < \min AVC\). Long-run exit: \(P < \min ATC\).

## Q6
meta: MCQ | 1 | B

Which one of the following is CORRECT in the context of a natural monopoly?

---

A **natural monopoly** exists when a single firm can supply the entire market at a lower average cost than two or more firms could. This happens when average cost keeps falling over the whole range of market demand, i.e. there are large economies of scale.

- **(C)** describes a legal (government-created) monopoly.

**Correct answer: (B)**

**Remember:** Natural monopoly = high fixed cost + falling average cost over the whole market.

## Q11
meta: MCQ | 2 | A
tags: Statistics | Hypothesis testing
In statistical hypothesis testing, the area of non-rejection is defined as ______.
(A) one minus probability of rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is true
(B) one minus probability of not rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is not true
(C) probability of rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is true minus probability of not rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is not true
(D) probability of rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is true
---
### Explanation from zero
In a hypothesis test, the possible values of the test statistic are split into two regions:
- **Rejection (critical) region:** if the statistic falls here, we reject \(H_0\).
- **Non-rejection (acceptance) region:** if it falls here, we do not reject \(H_0\).

The probability of falling in the rejection region **when \(H_0\) is true** is the **level of significance** (size), written \(\alpha\):
\[
\alpha = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ true}) = P(\text{Type I error}).
\]
The two regions together cover all outcomes, so under \(H_0\) the non-rejection region has probability
\[
1-\alpha = 1 - P(\text{rejecting } H_0 \text{ when it is true}),
\]
which is the confidence level.

Option (D) is \(\alpha\) itself (the rejection region). Option (B) is the **power** of the test.

**Correct answer: (A)**

**Remember:** Rejection region ↔ \(\alpha\); non-rejection region ↔ \(1-\alpha\).

## Q12
meta: MCQ | 2 | A
tags: Statistics | Mathematical statistics
If $\hat\beta$ is a consistent estimator of a population parameter $\beta$; $\varepsilon$ and $\delta$ are very small quantities, then ______.
(A) $\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{Prob}\left[|\hat\beta - \beta| < \varepsilon\right] \ge 1 - \delta$
(B) $\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{Prob}\left[|\hat\beta - \beta| < \varepsilon\right] = 0$
(C) $\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{Prob}\left[|\hat\beta - \beta| < \varepsilon\right] = \delta$
(D) $\lim_{n\to\infty}\text{Prob}\left[|\hat\beta - \beta| < \varepsilon\right] = 1 + \delta$
---
### Explanation from zero
An estimator \(\hat\beta\) is **consistent** if, as the sample size \(n\) grows, it gets arbitrarily close to the true parameter \(\beta\) with probability approaching 1. Formally, for every small \(\varepsilon>0\),
\[
\lim_{n\to\infty} P\left(|\hat\beta-\beta|<\varepsilon\right)=1.
\]
This is called **convergence in probability**: \(\operatorname{plim}\hat\beta=\beta\).

Saying the probability tends to 1 is the same as saying that, for any small \(\delta>0\), it eventually becomes at least \(1-\delta\). That is option (A).

- (B) and (C) say the probability goes to 0 or to a tiny number — the opposite of consistency.
- (D) is impossible because a probability can never exceed 1.

**Correct answer: (A)**

**Remember:** Consistency = the estimator's sampling distribution collapses onto the true value as \(n\to\infty\).

## Q13
meta: MCQ | 2 | B
tags: Statistics | Hypothesis testing
Power of a statistical test is defined as ______.
(A) one minus probability of rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is true
(B) one minus probability of not rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is not true
(C) probability of rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is true minus probability of not rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is not true
(D) probability of not rejecting the Null hypothesis when it is not true
---
### Explanation from zero
Two errors are possible in a test:
- **Type I error:** rejecting \(H_0\) when it is true. Probability \(\alpha\).
- **Type II error:** not rejecting \(H_0\) when it is false. Probability \(\beta\).

The **power** of a test is its ability to detect a false null hypothesis:
\[
\text{Power} = P(\text{reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ false}) = 1-\beta = 1 - P(\text{not rejecting } H_0 \text{ when it is not true}).
\]
- (D) is \(\beta\), the Type II error probability itself.
- (A) is \(1-\alpha\), the non-rejection probability under \(H_0\).

**Correct answer: (B)**

**Remember:** Power \(= 1-\beta\). A good test has small \(\alpha\) and high power.

## Q14
meta: MCQ | 2 | C
tags: Statistics | Hypothesis testing
If $Y = L^\alpha K^\beta$ is a Cobb-Douglas production function with two factors labor and capital, then the presence of constant returns to scale exhibited by the production function can be tested using ______.
(A) only $t$ statistic
(B) only $F$ statistic
(C) both $t$ and $F$ statistic
(D) $\chi^2$ statistic
---
### Explanation from zero
Taking logs turns the Cobb–Douglas function into a linear regression:
\[
\ln Y = \alpha\ln L + \beta\ln K \;(+\text{constant}+u).
\]
**Constant returns to scale** means doubling both inputs doubles output, which for Cobb–Douglas requires
\[
H_0:\ \alpha+\beta=1.
\]
This is a **single linear restriction** on the coefficients. It can be tested in two equivalent ways:
- **t-test:** \(t=\dfrac{\hat\alpha+\hat\beta-1}{SE(\hat\alpha+\hat\beta)}\), where \(SE\) uses \(Var(\hat\alpha)+Var(\hat\beta)+2Cov(\hat\alpha,\hat\beta)\).
- **F-test (restricted least squares):** compare the residual sum of squares of the restricted model (with \(\alpha+\beta=1\) imposed) and the unrestricted model.

For a single restriction the two tests give the same conclusion, because \(F = t^2\).

**Correct answer: (C)** — both \(t\) and \(F\) statistics.

**Remember:** One linear restriction → t or F (with \(F=t^2\)); several restrictions → F.

## Q15
meta: MCQ | 2 | A
tags: Statistics | Correlation and regression
In the context where sample mean of the dependent variable ($\bar Y$) lies closer to the observed $Y_i$ than its least square predictor ($\hat Y_i$), ______.
(A) the goodness of fit ($R^2$) is negative
(B) the goodness of fit ($R^2$) is equal to 1
(C) the goodness of fit ($R^2$) is equal to 0
(D) the goodness of fit ($R^2$) must lie between 0 and 1
---
### Explanation from zero
The goodness of fit is
\[
R^2 = 1-\frac{RSS}{TSS} = 1-\frac{\sum(Y_i-\hat Y_i)^2}{\sum(Y_i-\bar Y)^2}.
\]
- \(RSS\) measures how far the observations are from the model's predictions \(\hat Y_i\).
- \(TSS\) measures how far they are from the simple sample mean \(\bar Y\).

If the sample mean lies **closer** to the observed values than the regression predictions do, then
\[
RSS > TSS \quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{RSS}{TSS}>1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad R^2<0.
\]
In words, the model predicts worse than just using the average. With an intercept in an OLS regression this cannot happen, but it can when the model has no intercept (or is not estimated by OLS on the same data) — which is the situation described.

**Correct answer: (A)** — \(R^2\) is negative.

**Remember:** \(R^2<0\) means the model is worse than a horizontal line at \(\bar Y\).

---
### Explanation from zero
Banks keep a fraction of deposits as reserves (the **cash reserve ratio**, \(r\)) and lend out the rest. The loans are spent and re-deposited, so deposits multiply. The simple **money (deposit) multiplier** is

**Correct answer: (A)**

**Remember:** CRR ↑ → money multiplier ↓ → money supply ↓.

## Q18
meta: MCQ | 2 | A

According to the IS-LM model, if the central bank increases money supply, then the rate of interest will ______ and the income will ______.
(A) fall, rise
(B) rise, fall
(C) rise, rise
(D) fall, fall
---
### Explanation from zero

## Q20
meta: MCQ | 2 | D
tags: Microeconomics | Production and cost
Consider a firm that produces a single good using labour and capital. Let $C, Q, L, K, w, r$ and $p$ denote cost of production, level of output, labour, capital, wage rate, price of capital and the price of output, respectively. An example of the cost function for this firm is ______.
(A) $C = wL + rK$
(B) $C = \min\{wL, rK\}$
(C) $C = \dfrac{pQ}{wL + rK}$
(D) $C = Q\sqrt{rw}$

A **cost function** gives the **minimum cost** of producing a level of output \(Q\) when input prices are \(w\) and \(r\):
\[
C = C(w, r, Q).
\]
It depends only on **output and input prices**, not on the input quantities \(L\) and \(K\) (those have already been chosen optimally). A valid cost function is increasing in \(Q\), homogeneous of degree 1 in \((w,r)\) and concave in input prices.

- **(A)** \(wL+rK\) is the cost *identity* (expenditure on inputs). It depends on \(L\) and \(K\), not on \(Q\), so it is not a cost function.
- **(B)** and **(C)** are not meaningful cost functions (C even contains revenue \(pQ\)).
- **(D)** \(C = Q\sqrt{rw}\) depends on \(Q\), \(w\) and \(r\) only, rises with output and doubles when both input prices double. It is in fact the cost function of the Cobb–Douglas technology \(Q = 2\sqrt{LK}\): cost minimisation gives \(L = \tfrac{Q}{2}\sqrt{r/w}\), \(K = \tfrac{Q}{2}\sqrt{w/r}\), so \(C = wL + rK = Q\sqrt{rw}\).

**Correct answer: (D)**

**Remember:** Cost function \(= C(w, r, Q)\) — prices and output only.

## Q21
meta: MCQ | 2 | B
tags: Microeconomics | Consumer theory
Consider a consumer with the utility function, $U(x, y) = y + \sqrt x$, where $x$ and $y$ are quantity of two commodities consumed. Which one of the following is TRUE?
(A) Income elasticity of demand for both goods is 1.
(B) Income elasticity of demand for good $x$ is 0.
(C) Income elasticity of demand for good $y$ is 0.
(D) Income elasticity of demand for good $x$ is 0.5 and good $y$ is 1.
---
### Explanation from zero
Income elasticity measures how much demand changes when income changes.
\[
E_Y=\frac{\%\text{ change in quantity demanded}}{\%\text{ change in income}}
\]
The consumer's utility function is:
\[
U(x,y)=y+\sqrt{x}
\]
Suppose the prices of the goods are \(p_x\) and \(p_y\), and income is \(M\). The budget constraint is:
\[
p_x x+p_y y=M
\]
At an interior optimum the consumer equates the marginal rate of substitution with the price ratio:
\[
\frac{MU_x}{MU_y}=\frac{1/(2\sqrt{x})}{1}=\frac{p_x}{p_y}\quad\Rightarrow\quad x=\left(\frac{p_y}{2p_x}\right)^2.
\]
Since the utility function is **quasilinear**, the demand for \(x\) is independent of income once the consumer is at an interior solution. Thus, the income elasticity of \(x\) is **zero**. All additional income is spent on \(y\):
\[
y=\frac{M-p_x x}{p_y},\qquad E_y=\frac{dy}{dM}\cdot\frac{M}{y}=\frac{M}{M-p_x x}>1.
\]
So (A), (C) and (D) are false, and (B) is the only true statement.

**Correct answer: (B)**

**Remember:** Quasilinear utility → no income effect on the non-linear good.

## Q24
meta: MCQ | 2 | C
tags: Microeconomics | Imperfect competition
Consider the statements I and II in a monopolistically competitive market scenario. I. In the long-run equilibrium, the price of the good will be equal to the minimum of the average total cost. II. In the short run, firms may earn a positive profit. Which of the following options is CORRECT?
(A) Both I and II are TRUE
(B) I is TRUE but II is FALSE
(C) I is FALSE but II is TRUE
(D) Both I and II are FALSE
---
### Explanation from zero
**Statement I: False.**
In long-run equilibrium under monopolistic competition, firms earn zero economic profit. However, price generally does not equal the minimum point of average total cost because the firm faces a downward-sloping demand curve. Instead, the firm typically produces below the output that minimises average total cost. This is called **excess capacity**.

(A) Variance of the dependent variable ($Y_i$) is greater than the variance of the explanatory variable ($X_i$).
(B) The model is linear in both parameters and variables.
(C) The $Cov(Y_i, u_i) = 0$, where $u_i$ is the error term.
(D) The $Cov(X_i, u_i) = 0$, where $u_i$ is the error term.
---
### Explanation from zero
A regression model studies how one variable relates to another. For example:
\[
Y_i=\beta_0+\beta_1X_i+u_i
\]
- \(Y\): dependent variable.
- \(X\): explanatory variable.
- \(\beta_0,\beta_1\): parameters.
- \(u\): error term, representing other influences.

### Step 1: Read the function carefully
The paper prints \(y=x^{e^x}\): the variable \(x\) raised to the power \(e^x\). Both the base and the exponent change with \(x\), so neither the power rule nor the exponential rule applies directly. Use **logarithmic differentiation**.

### Step 2: Take logarithms
\[
\ln y=e^x\ln x
\]

### Step 3: Differentiate both sides
Using the product rule on the right:
\[
\frac{1}{y}\frac{dy}{dx}=e^x\ln x+e^x\cdot\frac1x
\]
\[
\frac{dy}{dx}=x^{e^x}\left(e^x\ln x+\frac{e^x}{x}\right)
\]

### Step 4: Substitute \(x=1\)
At \(x=1\): \(y=1^{e}=1\) and \(\ln1=0\), so
\[
\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=1}=1\times\left(e\cdot0+\frac{e}{1}\right)=e\approx\boxed{2.72}
\]

**Answer: 2.72** (the key accepts 2.70 to 2.72)

**Remember:** For \(f(x)^{g(x)}\), take logs first.
