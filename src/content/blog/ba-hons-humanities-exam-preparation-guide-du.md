Pursuing a BA (Honours) degree in Humanities—whether English, Political Science, History, or Sociology—at Delhi University requires a distinct approach to examinations. Unlike science or commerce, where answers are precise and numerical, humanities papers rely on subjective, argumentative essays.

Many students read the heavy weekly readings but struggle to convert that knowledge into high-scoring, 15-mark essays under a 3-hour time limit. 

This guide shares practical steps to structure your humanities essays and secure a high SGPA.

---

## 1. The Anatomy of a High-Scoring Humanities Essay

An outstanding subjective essay in a DU humanities exam must read like a research paper. It should showcase critical thinking, a deep understanding of the syllabus readings, and structural clarity. 

Follow this structure for all long-answer questions (12 to 15 marks):

### I. The Abstract / Conceptual Introduction (1 Page)
* **Goal:** Define the terms in the question and outline your central argument.
* **Tip:** If the question is about *Gramsci's Hegemony*, do not just define hegemony. Mention Antonio Gramsci’s background, the context of the *Prison Notebooks*, and the basic premise of cultural control.

### II. Historical & Contextual Framing (1 Page)
* **Goal:** Situate the debate in its historical or sociological context.
* **Tip:** Explain the dominant theories or ideas that existed before this concept arose, and why the new concept was necessary.

### III. The Core Argument & Textual Analysis (2-3 Pages)
* **Goal:** Present the main thesis points.
* **Tip:** Divide this section into clear subheadings. Cite primary readings. For example, if you are writing about Hobbes’s social contract, refer directly to *Leviathan*. 
* **Quote Scholars:** This is the most important scoring factor. Explicitly quote thinkers. E.g., *"As Rajni Kothari notes in 'Caste in Indian Politics'..."* or *"According to Romila Thapar's reading of Ashokan edicts..."*

### IV. Critical Evaluation & Debates (1 Page)
* **Goal:** Present counter-arguments and critiques.
* **Tip:** Humanities subjects are built on debates. Mention critiques of the theory. For instance, present feminist critiques of classical political theory, or Marxist perspectives on capitalist structures.

### V. Summary and Conclusion (Half Page)
* **Goal:** Reiterate your main points and offer a forward-looking perspective.

## 2. Reading Compilation vs. PYQ Prioritization

The weekly readings recommended by departments (often sold as "readings" at college photocopy shops or libraries) contain hundreds of pages of complex academic prose. Reading all of them in the final month before exams is practically impossible.

To save time:
1. Open the [DU PYQ archive](https://www.dupyq.online/pyq-notes) to pull the last four years of papers for your course.
2. Note down the 6–8 core questions that appear consistently.
3. Locate the *specific* readings that match those core questions.
4. Create **one page of outline notes** for each reading, containing:
   - The author's main thesis.
   - 3 to 4 core arguments.
   - 2 key direct quotes.
   - Common criticisms of the text.

---

## 3. Pacing Your Writing in the Exam Hall

Time management is the single biggest hurdle in humanities papers. Writing four 15-mark answers requires writing nearly 16–20 pages in 180 minutes. 

* **The 40-Minute Rule:** Allocate exactly 40 minutes per long question. Do not exceed this time, even if you have more points to write. A stellar first answer cannot make up for leaving the final question completely blank.
* **Maintain Readability:** Use a high-quality, comfortable pen. Examiners read hundreds of papers; if your handwriting is illegible, you risk losing marks, regardless of how insightful your arguments are.
