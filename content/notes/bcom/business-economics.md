# Business Economics

## Key Concepts

### Demand, Supply & Elasticity
- **Price Elasticity of Demand (Arc method)**: %Δquantity ÷ %Δprice, using averages of old/new values in denominator — tested directly as a numerical (shirt pricing example) followed by "elastic or inelastic?" and a pricing-strategy recommendation
- **Total Expenditure Method**: identify elasticity by observing whether total expenditure (Price × Quantity) rises, falls, or stays constant as price changes across a demand schedule — a favorite way DU tests elasticity conceptually without asking for direct calculation
- **"Other things remaining same" (ceteris paribus)** for the Law of Supply: technology, input prices, taxes/subsidies, expectations, number of sellers — must be explicitly listed when explaining the upward-sloping supply curve
- **Elasticity of Supply**: degrees (perfectly elastic, perfectly inelastic, unitary, relatively elastic/inelastic) explained via diagrams with different slopes through the origin

### Consumer Behaviour
- **Consumer's Equilibrium (Indifference Curve approach)**: tangency of highest attainable IC with the budget line; condition — MRS(xy) = Px/Py, and IC must be convex to origin at that point (diminishing MRS)
- **Non-convex indifference curves**: tested as "what if IC is concave to origin at equilibrium" — this represents an unstable/non-optimal point; perfect substitutes (straight-line IC) and perfect complements (L-shaped IC) are the standard special cases to discuss
- **Budget Line**: intercepts = Income/Price of each good; slope = −(Px/Py); shifts outward with income increase (parallel), rotates on price change of one good (holding the other axis intercept fixed)
- **Income vs Substitution Effect**: decomposing the total price effect for a normal good — substitution effect always increases quantity demanded when price falls (movement along same IC), income effect adds to it for a normal good (moving to a higher IC)
- **Lump-sum subsidy vs Excise (specific) subsidy**: a classic welfare-comparison diagram question — lump-sum subsidy (parallel shift of budget line) lets the consumer reach a higher indifference curve than an excise subsidy (rotation of budget line) for the same government cost, because it doesn't distort relative prices

### Production & Cost Theory
- **Isoquants & Isocost lines**: tangency gives the least-cost input combination (Producer's Equilibrium), condition MRTS = ratio of input prices (w/r)
- **Ridge Lines**: mark the boundary of the "economic region of production" where MP of both inputs is positive; outside ridge lines, one input's MP is negative — no rational producer operates there
- **Returns to Scale**: increasing/constant/decreasing, driven by economies/diseconomies of scale (internal: specialization, indivisibilities; external: industry-wide factors)
- **Expansion Path**: short-run (with one fixed factor, path is constrained/vertical-ish) vs long-run (all factors variable, smoother path through successive isoquant-isocost tangencies)
- **LAC as "envelope curve" and "planning curve"**: LAC is tangent to (envelopes) a family of SAC curves, one for each plant size; LMC is NOT an envelope of SMC curves — LMC passes through the point where each SAC touches LAC, but is derived differently (this exact distinction was asked directly)
- **MC-AC relationship**: MC intersects AC (and AVC) at their minimum points; when MC < AC, AC is falling; when MC > AC, AC is rising — tested as a numerical (given Total Cost schedule, compute MC/AC/AFC and identify the MC=AC output level)

### Market Structures
- **Perfect Competition**: Shut-down point in the short run = where price falls below minimum AVC (firm can't even cover variable costs); long-run equilibrium via entry/exit adjustment until P = min LAC
- **Monopoly**: equilibrium where MR = MC (short-run); supply curve is "indeterminate" under monopoly because there's no unique price-quantity relationship — the monopolist sets output from MR=MC then reads price off the demand curve, so the same quantity can correspond to different prices depending on demand elasticity
- **Monopolistic Competition**: long-run equilibrium at tangency of demand curve and LAC (zero economic profit), but NOT at LAC's minimum — this gap is the "excess capacity" concept, tested repeatedly across both papers
- **Oligopoly**: Kinked Demand Curve explains price rigidity (elastic above prevailing price for price increases, inelastic below for price cuts, creating a discontinuity in the MR curve that absorbs cost changes without price change); Collusive (cartel-like, joint profit maximization) vs Non-collusive (independent action, e.g. Cournot/Bertrand-style) distinction

### Pricing & Factor Markets
- **Third-degree price discrimination**: market segmented by elasticity (e.g. student vs regular pricing); conditions — seller must have market power, markets must be separable, and elasticity of demand must differ across segments; MR equalized across segments at the point equal to overall MC
- **First-degree price discrimination**: perfect discrimination, seller extracts entire consumer surplus by charging each unit at the maximum price a buyer is willing to pay
- **Peak-load pricing**: higher prices charged during peak demand periods to reflect higher marginal capacity cost, used in industries with non-storable output (electricity, transport)
- **Backward-bending labour supply curve**: beyond a threshold wage, substitution effect (favoring more work as wage rises) is outweighed by income effect (favoring more leisure as income rises), causing labour supply to fall as wages rise further
- **Minimum wage & Rent control**: classic price-floor/price-ceiling analysis; minimum wage above equilibrium creates a labour surplus (unemployment); rent control below equilibrium creates a housing shortage — short-run vs long-run elasticity differences amplify these effects over time
- **Nash Equilibrium**: strategy combination where no player benefits from unilaterally deviating, given the other's strategy — foundational to oligopoly behavior analysis

## Important Definitions (From Papers)

- **Paradox of Plenty (also called "Resource Curse")**: situation where an abundance of a resource does not translate to proportionate economic benefit, sometimes even harming overall economic performance — asked with a request for a "suitable business example"
- **Shut-down point**: the price level (= min AVC) below which a perfectly competitive firm stops production in the short run since revenue can't even cover variable costs
- **Excess Capacity (Monopolistic Competition)**: the gap between a firm's profit-maximizing output and the output at which LAC is minimized — firms operate below full capacity in long-run equilibrium
- **Kinked Demand Curve**: a demand curve with a "kink" at the prevailing price under oligopoly, explaining price rigidity/stickiness
- **Nash Equilibrium**: no player can improve their payoff by unilaterally changing strategy, given others' strategies remain fixed

## Practice Points

- **This is a pure theory + diagram paper, not a numericals paper** — opposite of Corporate Accounting. ~85% of marks require diagram-supported explanation/discussion; only 2 short numericals appear per paper (elasticity calculation, MC/AC/AFC schedule, or budget line intercepts)
- **Every question has a mandatory "OR"**: 5 questions × 9 marks × 2 parts (a/b or c/d), all compulsory — but within each numbered question, only ONE of the two lettered sub-parts (or the OR-alternative pair) needs to be attempted. Read the instructions carefully; the pattern differs slightly in phrasing between Hons and Prog papers but the mark structure (5 × 18 = 90) is identical
- **Near-identical structure Hons vs Prog**: both papers follow the same 5-question, same-topic-per-question skeleton (Q1=elasticity, Q2=consumer theory, Q3=production/cost, Q4=market structure, Q5=factor markets+short notes) — practicing one programme's papers is highly transferable to the other
- **Diagrams are not optional**: nearly every question explicitly says "with the help of a diagram" — a correct verbal explanation without the diagram will lose marks
- **Recurring numerical style**: demand-schedule tables (3 commodities, price column 12→2) and Total Cost schedules (5–6 output levels) are the standard numerical formats — practice reading off elasticity/MC swiftly from tabular data
- **Bilingual papers**: English + Hindi versions of every question printed together; Hindi numerical terms (जोड़ें/घटाएं, बजट रेखा, उदासीनता वक्र) are worth recognizing for quick cross-referencing
- **Short notes are a safe scoring zone**: the "write short note on (i)/(ii)" pairs at the end of Q5 are usually more time-efficient than a full diagram-based discussion — worth prioritizing if running short on time
